## Sprint 2 Review

For Sprint 2, our group continued our analysis of Skill Security and Supply-Chain Risk in Mining Software Repositories. Our research question remained unchanged: “How frequently do modified skills introduce new security risks, and do these modifications exceed what is necessary?” Our motivation is to see if copying without review creates a supply-chain risk. A modified copy of a widely reused skill could add command execution, file-system access, or network access that the original never had. In Sprint 1 we found 3,378 groups of identical artifacts (20,164 artifacts) and used RapidFuzz to pair same-name skills whose content differs. 
A competing explanation is that a new capability may be necessary because the skill's purpose or features changed. We therefore treat a new finding as a potential risk to be reviewed, not as proof of harm. 
As of Sprint 2, our study design is an analysis of a baseline–variant pair of same-name skills. The population is GitSkills skill artifacts. It is the approved GitSkills sample from the MSR 2027 challenge (agent_skills_sample.db). The primary outcome is the share of pairs in which the variant has a security-relevant scanner finding the baseline lacks.
 Our method pairs each modified skill with a same-name version, scan both with the same tool, and count rules that occur only the variant. First we find a “modified” skill by using name, file_sha, and first_commit_at. They are paired by RapidFuzz using a similarity score. Then we selected a range that eliminated near clones or entire skill changes. The artifacts are individually scanned by the security scanner. Non-security issues are filtered and the results are compared. Results are then summarized, and potential vulnerabilities can be reviewed.
The pipeline (src/test_cisco_gitskills.py) reads the approved Gitskills sample using DuckDB, selects the 188 near duplicate pairs produced by the Sprint 1 RapidFuzz comparison, and scans each of the 238 distinct artifacts once. It never executes skill content. It writes each skill to a temporary SKILL.md and runs only the scanner on it. Raw reports, per artifact results, group comparisons, and an error log are saved under results/. All 238 scans completed with zero errors.
Of 188 same-name skill pairs with 60–97% content similarity, in 5 pairs (2.7%) the variant had a security-relevant scanner finding that its baseline lacked. Commit dates are missing for most pairs, so which version came first is not confirmed. While the research question remained unchanged, we changed how we detect risk. Instead of our own keyword rules, we now run the Cisco AI Defense Skill Scanner on each skill and compare its findings between a baseline and its modified variant.
The main preliminary finding is that security findings are rare in this sample. Only 11 of the 238 aritfacts (4.6%) had any finding beyond the manifest metadata. The scanners output is mainly missing license notices (187 of 213 findings), which is not a security risk for artifact implementation, so we exclude manifest rules from the analysis. The five “new finding” pairs are candidates for manual review and not confirmed risks.



## Retrospective

While we kept our research question the same, we quickly realized that our initial method of keyword selection grew out of control quickly. Using a basic “set” of potential keyword vulnerabilities, we generated more than 6,600 results. Being able to use an “off the shelf” took care of how to isolate the keyword results. The addition of an established security scanner also helped with the defensibility of the results. Since we were using another artifact to scan with, we didn’t know how well it would work with the scope of the duplicate groups and same-name variants. We changed the unit of analysis from individual artifacts to baseline-variant pairs to capture modifications. A further reduction was based on the ideas that identical copies cannot add a capability, and very low similarity is likely a different skill. The reduction to 188 pairs worked great but should only be considered a starting point after reviewing the results. Modifications had to be made since most of the results were non-security related to modifications. The final results are a stepping stone for review of real vulnerabilities.
Security findings in the skill.md text of our sample set seem to be rare, so our artifact probably would need to be part of a bundle. Due to the number of missing commit dates, ensuring when changes are “introduced” is difficult. A much larger sample size may also generate more results. As of now, the sub-set of the same-name-variants csv is done by hand. We are looking to address some merge issues generated during creation that may be the cause. Next we plan to replicate results on bigger samples and try to dial in the variables that could undermine our results.
